# Coffee Roasting Fundamentals (fact sheet)

Coffee roasting transforms green coffee beans through a series of physical and chemical changes. Green beans contain roughly 8 to 12 percent moisture, which drops to about 1 to 2 percent by the end of roasting. The Maillard reaction, which begins around 140 to 165 degrees Celsius, produces several hundred aromatic compounds and the characteristic brown color.

First crack typically occurs when bean temperature reaches 196 to 205 degrees Celsius, as internal steam pressure ruptures the cell structure. Light roasts are usually dropped shortly after first crack begins, at bean temperatures around 205 to 215 degrees Celsius. Dark roasts proceed to second crack, around 224 to 230 degrees Celsius, when the bean surface turns oily.

Contrary to a popular belief, darker roasts contain slightly less caffeine by volume, because beans expand and lose mass during roasting; however, the difference between a light and a dark roast of the same bean is only about 5 percent or less when measured by mass. Chlorogenic acids, a major family of antioxidants in coffee, degrade by 50 to 100 percent depending on roast degree.

The roasting industry commonly uses the Agtron scale to quantify roast color, where higher numbers indicate lighter roasts; a typical filter-roast target sits around Agtron 55 to 65 for ground coffee. Global coffee production in the 2023/24 season was approximately 178 million 60-kilogram bags, with Brazil producing about 66 million bags and Vietnam about 30 million bags, together accounting for more than half of world output.

Espresso extraction is typically performed at 9 bar of pressure with water between 90 and 96 degrees Celsius, yielding 25 to 30 milliliters in 25 to 30 seconds for a single shot. Under-extracted espresso tastes sour because organic acids dissolve early, while over-extraction releases bitter compounds from the later stage of dissolution.
